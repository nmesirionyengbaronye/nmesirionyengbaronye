I am a Mechatronics Engineering student at FUTO building **systems that outlast their circumstances**.

Most people in my environment graduate looking for a job. I started building before I knew how to finish a single course properly. Not because I don’t take engineering seriously—because I believe the proof of engineering is in what survives real constraints, not what works on a fast connection in a lab.

## What I’ve Actually Built

### UniUI
**Live:** [app.uniui.com.ng](https://app.uniui.com.ng) · **Waitlist:** [waitlist.uniui.com.ng](https://waitlist.uniui.com.ng)

UniUI started because I watched students use AI that looked smart and was confidently wrong. I didn’t want another wrapper around an LLM. I wanted something that could say *I don’t know* and mean it.

That single requirement changed every technical decision:
- **Retrieval before generation.** Answers are grounded in actual course materials, not stochastic completion.
- **Sources before answers.** Every claim traces back to a document the student already has.
- **Confidence scoring before display.** The system shows uncertainty explicitly instead of hiding it behind fluency.
- **Explanation over memorization.** The goal is understanding, not a quick answer to paste into an exam.

The architecture is harder, the product is less flashy, and that’s the point.

I launched a waitlist, gathered early users physically across campus, tested course rep partnerships, and learned that building technology is not the same as distributing it. Flyers didn’t work. Course reps didn’t cooperate. A SUG post generated attention but wasn’t sustainable. I started thinking about distribution networks instead of advertising.

UniUI is no longer “ChatGPT for students.” It’s an **academic intelligence system** designed for environments where the internet disappears and the exam doesn’t.

### Pantero
**Status:** Testing · **Waitlist:** 190+ across Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, South Africa

Pantero started from a simpler observation: African students don’t have reliable internet, so cloud-dependent AI tools are already failing before they launch.

I moved the model to the browser.

The waitlist grew organically across four countries without paid ads. That wasn’t marketing. That was a real problem meeting a real constraint.

**What it does:**
- Runs AI mentorship directly in-browser using WebLLM
- No server dependency, no subscriptions, no data leaving the device
- Community and marketplace layer for African students
- Works on low-end smartphones and networks that drop every few hours
- Built by a student solving a problem he lived daily

The architecture mirrors what I learned from UniUI: **local-first, offline-capable, privacy-preserving**. The difference is the problem space—career mentorship instead of academic retrieval.

## How I Think About Engineering

I’m trained as a Mechatronics engineer. That means I think in systems: inputs, actuators, feedback loops, failure modes. Software is just another system with different failure modes.

### Constraint-Driven Design
If you design for ideal conditions, you haven’t designed anything. The best architectures are the ones that degrade gracefully under pressure: low bandwidth, low-end hardware, missing dependencies, user error.

Every project I build starts with the question: *What breaks when the network drops?* Not because I expect failure, but because I know my users experience it daily.

### Local-First Is Not a Preference
Cloud dependency is a luxury most of my users can’t afford. I design so the product works before it connects, and improves after it connects. This isn’t an ideological position—it’s a requirements document from my actual user base.

### Execution Over Explanation
I’ve written enough roadmaps to know that plans don’t build things. Code, shipped to real users, with real feedback, over time—that’s the only evidence that matters.

### Ambition Without Focus Is Just Noise
I’m interested in a lot of things: AI/ML, backend engineering, cybersecurity, Rust, systems programming, German, international opportunities, institutional technology policy. The skill I’m still learning is choosing what *not* to do, so what I do ship is durable.

### Engineering Discipline
As a Mechatronics student, I apply the same rigor to code that my field demands of physical systems: precision, reliability, and maintainability. A system that works once isn’t engineering. A system that works under pressure, repeatedly, with users depending on it—that’s engineering.

## The Real Story So Far

I didn’t start as a software engineer. I started as a Mechatronics student who kept asking why the tools around me didn’t work for people like me.

The internet at FUTO drops. The power goes out. Students use phones that cost less than a single textbook. AI tools assume connectivity, subscriptions, and devices that don’t exist here. I kept running into the same gap: technology built for people who don’t need it, ignoring the people who do.

So I started building for the gap.

UniUI was my first serious attempt. I bought the domain, built the waitlist, collected early users, launched V1, dealt with infrastructure problems, created a student community, tested course reps, gathered feedback physically, and gradually realized the product needed to be something different from what I originally imagined.

The pivot from “AI chatbot for students” to “academic intelligence system that knows when it might be wrong” was the hardest and most important design decision I’ve made. It meant giving up the flashy demo and building something less confident but more trustworthy.

Pantero came from the same frustration, applied to a different problem. African students need mentorship, but mentorship platforms assume connectivity and devices I know my users don’t have. Moving the AI to the browser wasn’t a feature—it was a requirement.

VitaChain is the third application of the same architecture to healthcare.

The pattern is: find a domain where existing solutions take infrastructure for granted, remove that dependency, and build something that works where it’s actually needed.

## Distribution Lessons

I’ve learned more from failed distribution than successful code.

- **Flyers don’t work.** I printed them, distributed them, and watched them disappear.
- **Course reps don’t cooperate consistently.** Even when the product is good, institutional friction kills organic growth.
- **Official posts generate attention but not retention.** The SUG post gave UniUI visibility. It didn’t give users who would stay.
- **Word of mouth across borders is real.** Pantero’s waitlist grew across Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, and South Africa without paid ads because the problem is continental, not campus-specific.

I’m now thinking about **distribution networks** instead of advertising. Ambassador structures, institutional partnerships, community builders who actually understand the product. The technology is necessary but not sufficient.
